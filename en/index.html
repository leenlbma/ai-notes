AI Notes · 01　/　2026

Still Someone Who
Builds Things by Hand

Notes on AI in 2026, from 16 years in the internet industry
Scroll to read ↓
010203040506

2026 isn't over yet, but it has been many years since a technology fascinated me this much.

I have worked in the internet industry for 16 years. I lived through the era of the traditional web and then mobile internet, and today I lead an AI team of nearly a hundred people, spanning models, algorithms, engineering, product, design, data, and AIGC content production.

By day I work on models, agents, AI infrastructure, and business delivery. By night I do my own Vibe Coding: building products, writing essays, open-sourcing small projects.

My strongest feeling this year: technological progress keeps pushing out the limits of what one person can do, but organizations, business, and people are changing far more slowly than the technology.

Some things have excited me, some have puzzled me, and some have made me rethink what I actually want to do with the next ten years.

01

For the first time, creating costs so little that I can't stop

What is truly scarce is not execution, but attention.

In the past, building a product, even from a simple idea, meant thinking about design, engineering resources, and schedules. Many ideas were not worthless; they just weren't worth mobilizing a whole team to test.

Now, a prototype can take a single evening.

This year I made a few open-source projects and tried quite a few products I was curious about. From a vague idea to interaction design, to code, to something that actually runs: much of the time, it took only me and AI.

These things may not have much commercial value, but I love the journey from idea to product.

After so many years in management, I have rediscovered the joy of writing code.

I used to spend most of my time organizing other people's work. Now I can do many things with my own hands.

What AI has really changed for me is not how much faster I write code. It is that a great deal of creation that would never have happened now has a chance to happen.

But the side effects are obvious too.

I find it harder and harder to stop.

Every day brings new models, new tools, new ideas. The time AI saves is quickly filled again by new projects.

The old problem was not having enough ability to realize my ideas. The new problem is having too many ideas worth trying.

I have begun to realize that what will be truly scarce in the future may not be the ability to execute, but attention, and the ability to decide what not to do.

What will be truly scarce may not be execution,
but attention, and the ability to decide what not to do.
02

Demos keep getting easier; real delivery is still hard

A model that can do a task once cannot necessarily do it reliably ten thousand times.

This year we put a lot of effort into models, agents, AIGC, and AI infrastructure.

Model capabilities are advancing fast and generation costs keep falling. Many features that once required complex engineering can now be done with a single model call.

But once you get to production, the problems become concrete.

Generating a video is not hard. The hard part is that the product must not be distorted, shots must stay consistent with one another, and when a client edits one part, nothing else may break.

Getting an agent to write code is not hard. The hard part is understanding the whole system, handling edge cases, and being able to locate and fix problems when they occur.

A model that can do a task once cannot necessarily do it reliably ten thousand times.

When I look at AI products now, I care less and less about how dazzling the demo is, and more about three questions:

Are the results reliable? Is the process controllable? Can it recover after failure?

There is another, easily overlooked problem: model capabilities are improving so fast that the lifespan of engineering solutions is shrinking.

A problem that needed elaborate prompts, workflows, and rule systems six months ago may, six months later, simply be handed to the model.

That means that while we build systems, we also have to keep overturning our own past technical judgments.

Engineering used to pursue stability and reuse. Now we also have to learn to judge what is worth building for the long term, and what is only a stopgap for what today's models can't yet do.

Are the results reliable? Is the process controllable?
Can it recover after failure?
03

Everyone got faster; the organization did not, at least not in proportion

Organizational efficiency is not simply the sum of individual efficiency.

This year I pushed my team to use AI across the board.

Engineers code with AI, product managers draft proposals with AI, designers explore interactions with AI, and algorithm researchers use AI to support their R&D.

The boost in individual productivity is palpable.

But a very practical question emerged:

If everyone has gotten faster, why are projects still late?

Eventually I realized that organizational efficiency is not simply the sum of individual efficiency.

An engineer finishing a week's worth of code in a day does not mean testing, review, integration, and release speed up along with it.

A product manager who comes up with ten proposals in a day may simply have given the team ten more options to evaluate.

As producing content gets cheaper, decision-making, acceptance, and coordination may become the new bottlenecks.

In the past we raised productivity through specialization, building functions like product, design, engineering, and QA, along with the processes and management mechanisms that support them.

But when AI can cross some of those professional boundaries, the old division of labor may no longer be optimal.

That is why I have spent this year thinking about what I call "Reorganizing."

Being AI-native does not mean giving every role an AI assistant. It means redesigning how work is defined, executed, accepted, and delivered.

If the organizational structure stays the same and everyone just gets one more AI tool, we may only be making the old process run busier.

If the organization stays the same…
we may only be making the old process run busier.
04

I have begun to reassess the value of experience and talent

AI has lowered the barrier to execution, but it has not erased the value of judgment.

Something else interesting happened this year.

A young engineer without much experience might, with AI's help, fix a complex bug in ten minutes.

We used to judge an engineer by coding speed and technical fluency. Those signals are starting to mislead.

When AI can generate code, proposals, and designs, how exactly should we judge what a person is really capable of?

I pay more and more attention to whether someone can understand the problem accurately, spot AI's mistakes, find the cause when results don't match expectations, and carry the final delivery through on their own.

AI has lowered the barrier to execution, but it has not erased the value of judgment.

It has also made me reexamine my own experience of the past decade and more.

Some technical experience really is depreciating fast, especially knowledge tied to particular tools, frameworks, and ways of implementing things.

But understanding of the business, systems thinking, product judgment, and the ability to break down complex problems may actually be amplified by AI.

Of course, experience can also become baggage.

Someone who always reads new technology through the methods that once made them successful can easily underestimate the change.

These days I would rather judge people by whether they can keep solving new problems than by how many problems they have solved before.

The same goes for me.

AI has lowered the barrier to execution,
but not the value of judgment.
05

The further technology advances, the slower organizations seem to change

Technological progress does not automatically bring organizational progress.

This year, some core members of my team left.

One received an offer that paid far more than their current salary and decided to keep growing somewhere new.

As their manager I was of course sorry to see them go, but I understood.

AI is changing the limits of what talented people can do, and it is changing how the talent market prices that ability.

Excellent people have more choices now, but organizations may not adjust their roles, incentives, and career paths in time.

It also made me start rethinking my own career choices.

Over the past decade and more I have been through plenty of business and organizational change, and I know that big companies inevitably carry coordination costs, competition for resources, and management problems.

But this year, I have grown less and less patient with certain things.

When technology has already given us the chance to redefine products and ways of working, it feels like a real waste to see so much time still spent on inefficient processes, internal politics, and meetings that decide nothing of substance.

Not everything needs to be faster.

Good products need polishing, hard technical problems need patience, and trust within an organization takes time.

I don't object to slowness. What I object to is waste that creates no value.

Technological progress does not automatically bring organizational progress.

A team can have the most advanced models and still collaborate in outdated ways; it can teach everyone to use AI without changing the mechanisms that actually determine how efficiently it delivers.

In the next phase, that may be a harder problem than getting the tools adopted.

I don't object to slowness.
I object to waste that creates no value.
06

I have begun to rethink what I really want to do

If I no longer measured myself by title and team size, what would I want to leave behind?

This year, beyond my team's work, I spent a good deal of time writing, building open-source projects, and studying products. I even started a plan to buy coffee for 100 people.

I wanted to meet people from different backgrounds and hear their real experiences of the AI era.

None of this may pay off much in the short term, but it exposed me to problems beyond my company's business, and it brought back my curiosity about products and people.

After more than a decade in the internet industry, it is easy to let promotions, pay, and the size of the team you manage define your career.

Those things matter, of course.

But this year I started to think seriously: if I no longer measured myself by title and team size, what would I want to leave behind in the next five or ten years?

I still love technology. I love building products with my own hands, and I love solving hard problems alongside excellent people.

I don't want a growing team to keep pulling me further from real technology and real users.

I also hope the experience I have built up will not just settle into one company's organization and systems, but gradually become my own products, work, methods, and lasting relationships.

That is something I started working on in earnest this year.

Not just settling into one company's organization and systems,
but gradually becoming my own products, work, methods, and lasting relationships.

⸻

Looking back on 2026, I am more optimistic than ever about the long-term future of AI, and more careful than ever about how I spend my own time.

I have seen model capabilities keep breaking through, and the uncertainty of production environments; individual creativity unleashed, and organizational collaboration becoming the bottleneck; how fast young people grow, and how past experience must keep being re-proven.

These are not conclusions drawn from launch events, leaderboards, or industry reports. They are impressions gathered day by day while writing code, building products, leading a team, and solving problems.

AI has given me back the joy of creating, and it has made me think seriously about what kind of work and life deserve my commitment.

When I started working 16 years ago, my greatest wish was to master the technology and do things well.

Sixteen years later, I find that I still love those same things.

It's just that now I have more choices, and I have begun to think more carefully about how to choose.

2026 isn't over yet.

I still write code every day. I still have many products I want to build, and many questions I haven't figured out.

But at least one thing is certain:

I hope my future self
will still be
someone who builds things by hand.
